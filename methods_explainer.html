The â€” character bug

Fixed. Those HTML files declare no character encoding, so when a browser can't find one it falls back to guessing — often Windows-1252 instead of UTF-8. An em dash (—) is stored as 3 UTF-8 bytes; read back as Windows-1252, those 3 bytes print as â€” instead. I added <meta charset="UTF-8"> as the first line of all 6 report HTML files (the two from today plus the four earlier ones), which tells the browser the correct encoding explicitly. Reopen them and the dashes should look right.

What kind of model is this?

Every model in model_results_25-74.csv is a linear mixed-effects model (LMM), fit with R's lme4::lmer(). "Linear" just means it's a regression — a weighted sum of predictors predicts the rating. "Mixed" means it has two kinds of effects living in the same model:

Fixed effectsThe things you actually care about estimating: FaceAge, CreativePerceptions, their interaction, Big Five traits, FaceSex. One estimate each, shared across everyone. These are the rows in the results CSV.
Random effectsWritten as (1 + FaceAge | Participant) and (1 | Face). These let the model absorb the fact that some participants just rate faces higher/lower overall (and some react more/less to age than others), and some faces just get rated higher/lower than others, without treating that as noise that contaminates the fixed effects you care about.

Why not just an ordinary regression (lm)? Because the data is trial-level — each of the ~47 participants rated 60 different faces, so there are ~2,800 rows in the model, but they aren't independent: participant 40's 60 ratings are all a bit correlated with each other (some people just rate everyone more generously), and every participant who saw "face #17" contributes a data point that's correlated with everyone else who also rated face #17. Ordinary regression assumes every row is independent, which would be false here and would make the p-values too optimistic (understated). The random effects for Participant and Face are exactly what corrects for that.

Why "crossed" random effects for Participant and Face?

Participants and Faces are crossed, not nested — every participant rates (mostly) the same pool of 60 faces, so "participant" and "face" aren't hierarchical categories (like students-within-classrooms), they're two independent groupings that cut across the same rows. (1 + FaceAge | Participant) + (1 | Face) tells lmer: give every participant their own baseline generosity and their own personal slope for how much FaceAge moves their ratings, and separately give every face its own baseline rating. The FaceAge × CreativePerceptions fixed effect is then estimated on top of all that person-level and face-level variation, rather than being confounded by it.

The convergence fallback ladder

The pre-registration commits to a specific fallback order if the fullest model won't fit cleanly. That's why model_results_25-74.csv has a spec_used column:

SpecRandom-effects formulaWhat changed
full(1 + FaceAge | Participant) + (1 | Face)Pre-registered structure, as-is.
no_slope_corr(1 + FaceAge || Participant) + (1 | Face)Keeps the per-participant FaceAge slope, but stops trying to also estimate how that slope correlates with each participant's baseline (fewer parameters).
no_slope(1 | Participant) + (1 | Face)Drops the idea that FaceAge affects different participants differently — everyone gets the same FaceAge slope, only baselines vary by participant.

In the 25–74 results: secondary_kai converged on the full spec (best case — the data supported the richest structure). The rest (primary_confirmatory, secondary_survey_score, exploratory) needed no_slope, and secondary_work_habits needed no_slope_corr. This isn't a red flag by itself — with ~47 participants there often just isn't enough data to reliably estimate individual-level slopes on top of everything else, so lmer gives up on that piece rather than reporting an unstable one.

Estimate, SE, t, and p — what each column means

Using the single most important row from the primary model as the running example:

FaceAge:CreativePerceptions   estimate = -0.197   SE = 0.048   t = -4.10   p < .0001
ColumnMeaning here
EstimateThe model's best guess at the size and direction of the effect, in the rating's own units (the slider is 0–100). -0.197 means: for every 1-point increase in a participant's Creative Perceptions score, the effect of 1 year of face age on their rating drops by 0.197 points. It's a slope-on-a- slope — how much the FaceAge slope itself shifts as CreativePerceptions goes up.
SE (Standard Error)How uncertain that estimate is — the typical amount the estimate would bounce around if you reran the study with a fresh sample from the same population. Small SE relative to the estimate means the estimate is precise; here 0.048 is small relative to -0.197, so the model is fairly confident about both the sign and roughly the size.
t (t-statistic)Just Estimate ÷ SE (-0.197 / 0.048 ≈ -4.10). It answers "how many standard errors away from zero is this estimate?" As a rule of thumb, |t| above ~2 is usually notable; here it's over 4, which is a lot of standard errors from zero.
p (p-value)The probability of seeing a t this extreme (or more extreme) if the true effect were actually zero — i.e., if Creative Perceptions had nothing to do with the age-advice relationship. p < .0001 means that would be extremely unlikely. It is not "the probability H1 is true" — it's a statement about how surprising this data would be under the null hypothesis of no effect.

One wrinkle specific to this kind of model: df (degrees of freedom, also in the raw CSV, e.g. 2713 for that row) isn't a clean integer like in a t-test you'd compute by hand. lmerTest (loaded alongside lme4 in the Rmd) uses the Satterthwaite approximation to estimate an effective sample size for each fixed effect, accounting for the random-effects structure. You don't need to do anything with this number — it's what lmerTest uses internally to compute the p-value — but it's why you'll see decimals like 40.575 instead of a round number.

The Creative Perceptions scale — what it's out of

From frontend/src/html/creative-perceptions.html and the scoring section of the Rmd:

  • 11 items, each a statement about whether society/skills/work are shifting toward the "creative class" (e.g. "Workplaces today increasingly value creativity and original thinking over following established procedures", "Finding unique ideas or solutions to problems will be more important for success in 2050 than it is today").
  • 5-point agree/disagree scale per item: Strongly Disagree = 1, Disagree = 2, Neither Agree nor Disagree = 3, Agree = 4, Strongly Agree = 5.
  • 2 reverse-worded items (Q6: "the way people work and succeed... has not changed much," Q11: "jobs will not require substantially greater knowledge") are flipped via 6 - x before averaging, so agreeing with a "nothing is changing" statement counts as a low creative-perceptions score, same direction as everything else.
  • Final score = the mean of all 11 (post-reversal) items — not a sum — so it lands back on the original 1–5 scale: 1 = strongly perceives no societal shift toward the creative class, 5 = strongly perceives that shift as real, fast, and already reshaping what success requires.

In the 25–74 sample, scores clustered around ~4.0–4.5 (e.g. participant 25 scored 4.45, participant 26 scored 4.09) — meaning most people in this batch lean toward agreeing the creative class is rising, not a scale spread evenly across 1–5. That matters for interpreting the interaction: the -0.197 per-point effect is being multiplied mostly by values clustered near the top of the scale, not the full 1–5 range, which is part of why the concrete numbers in the earlier report (slope ≈ 0.71 at CP = 2 vs. ≈ 0.23 at CP = 4.4) used those realistic, observed values rather than the scale's theoretical endpoints.
